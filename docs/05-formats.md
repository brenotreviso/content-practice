# 5 · Formats

> **In one sentence:** a format has three layers — where it lives (the container), how it is made (production), and what kind of story it is (content type) — and you can mix them.

Maya can post a “come with me” canal walk (content type) as a compilation with voiceover (production) in a Reel (container). Those are three different choices.

## New words

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Container** | The box the platform gives you: a Reel, a TikTok, a Short, a long YouTube video, a carousel, a Story, a post. |
| **Production format** | How the pictures and sound are made. |
| **Content type** | What the video is *doing* in the viewer’s life (a vlog, an explainer, a “come with me”). |
| **Voiceover** | Speech recorded over pictures, rather than spoken on camera. |
| **Safe area** (or safe margin) | The part of the frame not covered by buttons, captions or your chin. |

Exact length limits and button names change. See the [platform snapshot](platform-snapshot.md).

## Containers (where it lives)

| Container | What it is, in brief | Official starting points |
|---|---|---|
| **Short video** (TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts) | A vertical video made for a swipe feed. | Instagram, [Reels](https://about.instagram.com/features/reels); YouTube, [Shorts editing tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13380879?hl=en) |
| **Long-form YouTube** | A horizontal or vertical video on YouTube that is not a Short. Titles and thumbnails matter more because people often choose it from a grid. | [Thumbnail & title tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en) |
| **Carousel** (Instagram, sometimes LinkedIn or TikTok photo mode) | Several stills a viewer swipes through. Good when motion would add nothing. | Instagram Help on posts and captions: [Add a caption before sharing your post on Instagram](https://help.instagram.com/236245819849257) |
| **Stories** | Usually 24-hour posts. Useful for process, not always for the “main” idea. | Treat as a container; check current duration in the app. |
| **A regular post** (X, Instagram, LinkedIn, Threads) | Text, a still, or a short clip in a feed that is not only a video app. | See [chapter 10](10-platforms.md) |

**When to use which, as a beginner:** start with short video on **one** platform. Add long-form YouTube when you have something that needs more than a minute. Use a carousel when the idea is a list of stills (a mending sequence, a recipe card). Do not open five containers in week one.

## Production formats (how it is made)

For each format below: a definition, when to use it, how to make it, a real reference where we have one, and Maya’s version. Real accounts were checked as live channels or official pages on 20 Nov 2026. We did not invent creators. Where we could not honestly pin one canonical video, we say so and use official how-tos plus Maya.

### Collage / compilation with a voiceover

**Definition.** Several short clips, cut together, with you talking over them rather than (or as well as) on camera.

**When to use it.** You want to show a process (a meal, a walk, a mend) and you do not want, or are not ready, to talk to camera. Also useful when the pictures would be silent without explanation.

**How to make it.** Film more clips than you need, each a few seconds. In the app, put them in order, trim the dead air, then record a voiceover to the cut. Leave a little quiet at the ends. One idea per video.

**Real reference.** This is a very common pattern rather than one person’s invention. For a clearly sourced *talking-about-pictures* cousin, science and education channels often cut process or field footage under a narration; [Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/@veritasium) (Derek Muller) is a long-running example of explanation over demonstrated footage, not a “Day in the life” compilation. We are pointing at the channel, which we opened, not claiming every video uses this exact cut.

**Maya.** Her first video: chopping, the pan, the plate, voiceover. No face required.

### Static image with text on screen and a song

**Definition.** One still (or a very slight move), words on screen, music underneath. No, or almost no, new filming.

**When to use it.** You have one sentence worth saying, a notebook line, a recipe card, or a “here is the rule I keep forgetting”. It is also a low-energy option on a busy week.

**How to make it.** Use a clear photo you took. Add short text (see [chapter 8](08-text-hashtags-thumbnails.md) and Mayer’s caution about stacking text + voice + picture in [chapter 6](06-attention.md)). Pick a licensed song in the app’s library, not a random MP3. Keep the text large enough to read on a phone.

**Real reference.** Official tools: Instagram, [Add text and text-to-speech to your reel](https://help.instagram.com/3544819209117630); YouTube, [Shorts editing tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13380879?hl=en). We did not pick a single canonical creator for “quote card plus song”, because the pattern is generic and we will not invent a “best account”.

**Maya.** A photo of her notebook and one line: “Shop once, cook twice.” A short song from Instagram’s library. Twenty seconds.

### Talking to camera / yapping

**Definition.** You, on camera, speaking directly. “Yapping” is informal: thinking out loud, not a news read.

**When to use it.** The point is your take, a story, or a clear explanation. Also when showing a process would actually hide the idea.

**How to make it.** Phone at eye height, camera in landscape or portrait to match the container, light in front of you. Look at the lens, not the preview. One take if you can; a jump cut if you must. Write the first and last sentence, improvise the middle if that sounds more like you.

**Real reference.** [YouTube’s Creator Insider channel](https://www.youtube.com/@creatorinsider) is YouTube’s own talking-to-camera series about the product (the channel describes itself as informal communication from the creator product team, not a Help Centre policy page). [Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/@veritasium) often speaks to camera and then cuts to a demonstration.

**Maya.** She postpones this until video three, then films 25 seconds at the kitchen table, script in hand for the first line only.

### Visual aids

**Definition.** You support a talking point with a prop, a sketch, a list, a pan, a stitch, or a simple demonstration.

**When to use it.** Anytime “show, don’t only tell” would make the idea easier. Especially for explainers.

**How to make it.** Put the object close enough to see. Hold it still. If you write a list, write few words. Do not decorate the frame until the aid is readable.

**Real reference.** [Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/@veritasium) regularly uses props, diagrams and in-world demonstrations next to a spoken explanation. [Kurzgesagt – In a Nutshell](https://www.youtube.com/@kurzgesagt) is a different production path — illustrated explainers, with an official site at [kurzgesagt.org/who-we-are](https://kurzgesagt.org/who-we-are) — useful if your “visual aid” is animation rather than a prop (and if you are making that animation with code, see [Coded Motion](https://github.com/brenotreviso/coded-motion)).

**Maya.** She holds up the tin of tomatoes and the single pan. That is a visual aid.

### Image overlays

**Definition.** Photos, screenshots or graphics placed over or beside the main footage.

**When to use it.** You need to show a label, a still of a stitch, a screenshot of a question (with permission / no private data), or a before-and-after.

**How to make it.** Keep overlays large and on screen long enough to read. Do not cover faces or the thing you are talking about. Do not paste other people’s copyrighted frames as if they were yours.

**Real reference.** Official text/overlay tools: Instagram and YouTube Shorts Help as above. No single canonical “overlay creator” is named here.

**Maya.** A close-up still of a darned sock over a wide shot of the sock on her lap.

### Green screen

**Definition.** You (or a cut-out of you) placed over other footage or a still, usually with the app’s effect, not necessarily a physical green cloth.

**When to use it.** You are reacting to or explaining an image, a map, a still from your own video, or another piece of media you have the right to use.

**How to make it.** TikTok’s Creator Academy article [Creating green screen content](https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/article/creating-green-screen-content) (opened 20 Nov 2026; the page listed a 2026 update) walks through a plain background or a real green screen, even front lighting, a stable camera, and high-resolution backgrounds that fill a 9:16 frame. TikTok Newsroom also has [How to use the green screen effect](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/green-screen-effect-on-tik-tok/). Use media you have rights to.

**Maya.** She tries this later, with her own photo of the canal behind a short talking-head take. She does not green-screen a TV show she does not own.

### Reacts

**Definition.** You watching or responding to another piece of media in the same video.

**When to use it.** You have something of your own to add — a correction, a how-to, a feeling — not only your face next to someone else’s work.

**How to make it.** Use the platform’s own tools (TikTok and Instagram have official ways to stitch or remix, which change; check the app). Credit the source. Keep your addition longer than a shrug. Be careful with copyright; official guidance beats a guess.

**Real reference.** We are not going to crown a “best react creator”. Use official tools and your own point of view. If you cannot add anything, skip the format.

**Maya.** She mostly skips reacts at the beginning. If she uses one, it is her own earlier video: “Here is what I did wrong last Tuesday.”

## Content types (what the video is)

### Day in the life and vlogs

**Definition.** A stretch of real time, edited to a shape — morning to evening, or one outing — with a point, not a dump of clips.

**When to use it.** The value is being *with* you through a day that has a theme (a quiet Sunday, a shop-and-cook day), not watching a highlight reel with no spine.

**How to make it.** Decide the theme before you film. Shoot more than you use. Narrate only what the pictures cannot say. End on the payoff of the theme.

**Real reference.** [Casey Neistat](https://www.youtube.com/CASEYNEISTAT)’s daily vlogs are a well-known long-form example of this type (his official site, [caseyneistat.com](https://www.caseyneistat.com/), links that YouTube channel). The style is his; do not copy his life, copy the idea of a day with a shape. Dates and posting habits change; we are pointing at a real, checked channel, not a current view count.

**Maya.** A “shop, mend, cook” Sunday, six clips, a voiceover, under a minute. Not a full-day documentary.

### Outfit of the day (OOTD)

**Definition.** What you are wearing, and why it might matter to the viewer — not only a mirror turn.

**When to use it.** Clothes are one of your pillars, and you have one useful point (how you mended this, why this works for a commute, how a charity-shop find fits).

**How to make it.** One outfit, one reason, a close-up of the interesting bit (a mend, a hem, a shoe you actually walked in). Say the why in the first seconds.

**Real reference.** The name of the format is in common use; we did not pick a single canonical fashion account, to avoid inventing a “best OOTD creator”. Official text and Reel tools are enough to make one honestly.

**Maya.** The darned jumper, why she kept it, ten seconds of walking in it. Not a haul.

### Come with me / spend the day with me

**Definition.** The viewer is invited along on one outing or task.

**When to use it.** Movement helps: a shop, a walk, a library, a kitchen from fridge to plate. The “come with” is a promise of company, not of luxury.

**How to make it.** Film from your point of view or just behind you. Keep the outing small. Narrate lightly. End when the task ends.

**Real reference.** Again a widespread pattern. Maya’s canal walk is the worked example in this guide. We would rather not invent a “top come-with-me account”.

**Maya.** “Come with me on the 20-minute canal loop I do after work.” Compilation plus voiceover.

### Explainers and education

**Definition.** You teach one thing clearly, then stop.

**When to use it.** You can make someone more able to do or understand a thing after 30–90 seconds (or longer, on YouTube).

**How to make it.** One question, one answer, one demonstration. No syllabus in a Short.

**Real reference.** [Kurzgesagt – In a Nutshell](https://www.youtube.com/@kurzgesagt) makes illustrated science explainers (see [who they are](https://kurzgesagt.org/who-we-are)). [Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/@veritasium) often starts from a question and demonstrates the answer. These are high-production references, not a standard you must meet in week one.

**Maya.** “How I stop burning onions”: one mistake, one fix, the pan as a visual aid.

### Curiosity

**Definition.** You open a question or a gap the viewer wants closed. This is often a *type of opening*, and can sit on top of an explainer, a vlog or a compilation.

**When to use it.** When the honest hook is a question you will actually answer. See [chapter 6](06-attention.md) and the curiosity-gap template in [chapter 7](07-scripting.md).

**How to make it.** Ask or imply a specific question early. Do not delay the answer until it feels like a trick. Close the loop.

**Real reference.** Veritasium’s videos are often built around a question the viewer may have got wrong; watch any recent upload on [the channel](https://www.youtube.com/@veritasium) and notice where the question is asked and where it is paid off. That is a craft observation, not a claim about their analytics.

**Maya.** “Why does this tin-of-tomatoes dinner taste flat — and what one thing fixes it?”

## How to choose, quickly

Ask:

1. What is the one promise?
2. Do I need my face, or do hands and a voice do it?
3. Is this one task (come with me), one lesson (explainer), one question (curiosity), or a stretch of time (vlog)?
4. Which container am I posting to this week?

If you cannot answer those, you are not short of formats. You are short of a promise.

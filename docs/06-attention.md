# 6 · Attention: why people stop and stay

> **In one sentence:** getting someone to stop scrolling and getting them to stay are two different jobs, and both are limited by how much a mind can process at once.

This chapter is the theory under formats and scripts. It uses real studies. **These studies were usually not measured on Instagram Reels.** Where a finding comes from a lab, a film clip, or a classroom, we say so, and we do not pretend it is a law of TikTok.

## New words

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Attention** | The limited capacity to take in some things and not others. |
| **Cognitive load** | How much of that capacity a message is using. |
| **Novelty** | Something that is new or unexpected, relative to what just happened. |
| **Curiosity** | The urge to fill a gap in what you know. |
| **Retention (platform)** | The official graph of how much of a video people watched. |
| **Retention (memory)** | What people remember later, which is not the same graph. |
| **Peak-end rule** | A finding that people often remember an experience by its most intense moment and its ending. |

## How attention works: you only have so much

A useful starting point is that attention is **limited**. Daniel Kahneman’s book *Attention and Effort* (1973) is a classic statement of that idea, and later media research builds on it.

For *media* specifically, Annie Lang’s **Limited Capacity Model of Mediated Message Processing** (often shortened to LC4MP after motivation was added) says people have a limited pool of resources that are automatically allocated to **encoding**, **storing** and **retrieving** information in a message. The earlier version appeared as [The Limited Capacity Model of Mediated Message Processing](https://academic.oup.com/joc/article-abstract/50/1/46/4110103) (*Journal of Communication*, 2000). Later overviews of LC4MP include an encyclopedia entry, [Limited Capacity Model of Motivated Mediated Message Processing (LC4MP)](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781118783764.wbieme0077).

**What this means in a kitchen, not a lab:** if your video asks someone to read a paragraph, listen to a voiceover, follow a fast cut, *and* decode a joke, something will drop. You cannot “add more information” for free.

## Stopping versus staying

Two jobs:

1. **Stop** — notice this video rather than the next swipe.
2. **Stay** — find the next seconds worth the time.

A [2024 CHI conference study](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642433) ([open arXiv copy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04945), same paper) analysed TikTok use from **data donations**: 347 users and 9.2 million recommended videos. The authors reported that 45% of views were watched to the end, 24% were skipped before 20% of the video, and 40% were skipped before halfway. That is evidence that drop-off is often **front-loaded** on that platform, in that sample — not a universal law, and not a recipe for a hook length.

Marketing phrases such as “thumb-stop rate” are common in vendor blogs. Unless we can open a study that actually measures the phrase, treat those claims as **unverified**.

## Novelty

D. E. Berlyne’s book *Conflict, Arousal, and Curiosity* (1960) is a foundational account of how **novelty**, complexity, uncertainty and incongruity can spark curiosity and exploration. Later writers summarise his “collative variables” as things the mind compares with what it already expects. Too little is dull; too much is noise.

**For a video:** a new first frame, an unexpected first line, or a picture that does not match the last five swipes can help with *stopping*. A constant barrage of novelty can raise load and hurt *staying*.

## Curiosity

George Loewenstein, in [The Psychology of Curiosity](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75) (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1994), describes curiosity as **cognitively induced deprivation**: you notice a gap between what you know and what you could know, and you want it filled. A later review, [The psychology and neuroscience of curiosity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4635443/), discusses this information-gap account and cites Kang and colleagues (2009), who found that curiosity about trivia answers was highest when people had some idea but were not sure — a U-shape with confidence.

**For a video:** open a *specific* gap you are actually going to close. “You won’t believe this” is a gap with no content. “I kept burning the onions — here is the one thing I changed” is a gap with a payoff. Chapter 7 has a fill-in template.

## Emotion

LC4MP also talks about **motivated** processing: emotionally relevant material changes how resources are allocated. Separately, the **peak-end rule** comes from work by Barbara Fredrickson, Daniel Kahneman and colleagues: people often judge an episode by a combination of its most intense moment and its ending, and they can neglect duration. Two 1993 papers are [Duration Neglect in Retrospective Evaluations of Affective Episodes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8355141/) (Fredrickson & Kahneman, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) and [When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x) (Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier, *Psychological Science*).

**For a video:** the ending is not a leftover. A clear payoff, or a quiet accurate last line, is part of how the video will be *remembered* — which is not identical to the platform’s retention graph. These papers used film clips and other lab tasks, not Reels.

## Cognitive load, and text on screen

John Sweller’s [Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning](https://doi.org/10.1016/0364-0213%2888%2990023-7) (*Cognitive Science*, 1988) is a foundational paper on **cognitive load** in learning.

Richard E. Mayer and Roxana Moreno applied related ideas to multimedia. Their **Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning** includes, among others:

- **Modality principle:** people often learn better from pictures plus *spoken* words than from pictures plus on-screen text (see [Cognitive principles of multimedia learning: The role of modality and contiguity](https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-0663.91.2.358), Moreno & Mayer, 1999).
- **Redundancy principle:** adding on-screen text that *duplicates* a narration, on top of animation, can hurt. Mayer and Moreno summarise this in [Nine Ways to Reduce Cognitive Load in Multimedia Learning](https://faculty.washington.edu/farkas/WDFR/MayerMoreno9WaysToReduceCognitiveLoad.pdf) (2003).

**Important limits:** these are mostly educational-multimedia studies, not short-form social video. Also, many social videos are watched **muted**, so captions are still needed for access. The practical translation is not “never use captions”. It is:

- Do not stack a dense voiceover, a dense paragraph on screen, *and* a busy picture.
- Captions should usually *be* the spoken words, short enough to read, not a second essay.
- If the picture is doing the teaching, let the words be fewer.

Chapter 8 applies this to captions and thumbnails.

## Retention: two meanings, and a disputed favourite fact

**Platform retention** is a watch-time curve in YouTube Studio, TikTok Studio or Instagram insights. Learn to read it in [chapter 11](11-analytics.md).

**Memory retention** is what people can recall later. The **Zeigarnik effect** — Bluma Zeigarnik, 1927 — is often quoted as “unfinished tasks are remembered better than finished ones.” The APA Dictionary has a short entry: [Zeigarnik effect](https://dictionary.apa.org/zeigarnik-effect). A 2025 meta-analysis, [Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w), found **no reliable memory advantage** for unfinished tasks across the studies it synthesised, while the related **Ovsiankina effect** (a tendency to *resume* unfinished tasks, 1928) had more consistent support.

**What we will say in this guide:** “open loops” are a useful *craft* (ask a question, pay it off). We will not tell you that science has proved unfinished videos are always better remembered. The memory claim is disputed; the craft can still be done honestly.

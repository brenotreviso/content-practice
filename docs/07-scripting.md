# 7 · Scripting and copywriting

> **In one sentence:** hook, body and CTA are a useful skeleton, but the reason a skeleton works is psychology and story craft — and you can borrow those as fill-in structures without pretending they are laws.

A longer catalogue, with a worksheet for each structure, lives in [templates/script-templates.md](templates/script-templates.md). Maya fills one in first; you copy the blank.

Copy frameworks that began as sales formulas (AIDA, Problem–Agitate–Solve) are taught here as **attention structures only**. There is no product to sell in this guide.

## New words

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Script** | The words you plan to say or show, even if you then speak more loosely. |
| **Hook** | The opening that earns the next seconds. |
| **Payoff** | Where the opening promise is kept. |
| **CTA** | A next step for the viewer. Here: try, reply, watch, save — never buy. |
| **Framework** | A reusable sequence of moves (promise then payoff, or problem then insight). |
| **Open loop** | A question or thread you start and have not yet closed. |

## A simple skeleton, still worth knowing

For a short video:

1. **Hook** — what this is, and why it is worth a moment.
2. **Body** — one problem, one detail, one step.
3. **Payoff** — the kept promise.
4. **CTA (optional)** — one soft next step.

Write the hook and the payoff first. If they do not match, the script is dishonest. YouTube’s official [thumbnail and title tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en) already say titles should accurately represent the video; the same rule applies to the first sentence.

## The catalogue

For each framework: what it is, the theory and sources, a real or official example where we have one, a limit or caution, and a fill-in shape. Anything whose origin is messy is marked.

### 1. Curiosity gap (information gap)

**Theory.** Loewenstein (1994): curiosity as a felt gap between what you know and what you could know ([doi:10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75)). See [chapter 6](06-attention.md).

**Example.** [Veritasium](https://www.youtube.com/@veritasium) often opens on a question the viewer may have got wrong, then demonstrates the answer. Watch a recent video and mark where the question is asked and where it is paid off — a craft observation, not a claim about their numbers.

**Fill in.**

> You probably think ________.  
> I thought that too, until ________.  
> Here is what actually happens: ________.  
> So, if you try this, do ________.

**Maya.** “You probably think a Tuesday dinner needs a long recipe. I thought that too, until I started cooking from one tin and one pan. Here is what actually happens…”

### 2. Open loops

**Theory / craft.** Starting a thread and delaying the close. People often justify this with the Zeigarnik effect. As [chapter 6](06-attention.md) says, a 2025 meta-analysis did **not** find a reliable memory advantage for unfinished tasks ([s41599-025-05000-w](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w)). Use the loop as craft; do not claim the science is settled.

**Caution.** A loop you never close is a tease. Close it in the same video unless you have a clearly marked series.

**Fill in.**

> Before I show you ________, notice this one thing: ________.  
> (middle of the video)  
> Here is that thing, and why it mattered: ________.

### 3. Expectation violation

**Theory.** Judee K. Burgoon’s **Expectancy Violations Theory** started as a theory of interpersonal and nonverbal behaviour, not of Reels. Key paper: [A communication model of personal space violations](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.1978.tb00603.x) (*Human Communication Research*, 1978). Later work, including [1993](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0261927X93121003), widened it. The core claim is that a departure from what is expected *draws* attention, and then is judged as positive or negative.

**Caution.** Do not over-claim. A surprise that feels cheap or mean is a negative violation. Translate carefully.

**Fill in.**

> You would expect ________.  
> Instead, ________.  
> That matters because ________.

**Maya.** “You would expect a nicer meal to need more ingredients. Instead, the thing that improved mine was cooking the onions longer, not adding more food.”

### 4. Pattern interrupt (the edit, not the NLP theory)

**Craft.** A change of shot, sound, or text that breaks a repeat — a cut to the pan, a word on screen, a sudden quiet.

**Caution.** The *name* is also used in neuro-linguistic programming (for example Bandler and Grinder, *Frogs into Princes*, 1979), often linked to Milton Erickson. That tradition is **not** scientific consensus. This guide teaches the edit. It does not teach NLP or hypnosis.

**Fill in.** (This is a shooting note more than a monologue.)

> At second ____, change: shot / sound / text.  
> The change is there to wake up the next line: ________.

### 5. Promise, break, frustrate, solve

**Status.** This is the pattern Breno described for the guide: make a promise, break an expectation, let the tension sit, then give the solution. **Unverified as a formal published title** — we have not found this exact four-step name as a single established framework. We teach it as a usable structure and map it onto the sourced ideas above (curiosity gap, expectation violation, delayed payoff) and onto Problem–Agitate–Solve, below.

**Fill in.**

> Promise: by the end you will know ________.  
> Expected path: most people would ________.  
> That’s where it usually goes wrong: ________.  
> The fix: ________.  
> (Optional CTA): if you try it, tell me ________.

**Maya.** Promise: you will be able to cook the tomato pan without the bitter onions. Expected path: rush them on a high heat. Where it goes wrong: they burn and the whole pan tastes of that. Fix: lower heat, more time, a pinch of salt early.

### 6. But / therefore (not “and then”)

**Source.** Trey Parker and Matt Stone, in an NYU writing class filmed for mtvU’s *Stand In* (2011), argued that story beats should be connected with **“but”** or **“therefore”**, not a string of “and then”. A commonly cited upload is [Writing Advice from Matt Stone & Trey Parker @ NYU](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vGUNqq3jVLg). We opened the YouTube watch page on 20 Nov 2026; as with any unofficial upload of a class, treat the wording as their recorded advice, not a peer-reviewed paper.

**Fill in.** List your beats, then rewrite every “and then” as “but” or “therefore”.

> I did ________, **but** ________, **therefore** ________.

**Maya.** “I bought more ingredients, *but* the dinner still tasted flat, *therefore* the problem was the onions, not the shopping list.”

### 7. Problem–Agitate–Solve (PAS)

**What it is.** Name the problem, heighten it, then offer the insight or fix.

**Origin.** An established direct-response copy formula. **Inventor unverified.** You will see it attributed to various famous copywriters; we did not find a solid first publication to name with confidence, so we will not invent one. A widely read description is Demian Farnworth’s [Copyblogger piece on Problem-Agitate-Solve](https://copyblogger.com/problem-agitate-solve/) (2014), which says he learned it from an unnamed newsletter archive.

**Caution.** In sales, the “solve” is often a product. Here the solve is the thing you are teaching. Do not agitate in a way that shames the viewer.

**Fill in.**

> Problem: ________.  
> Why it nags: ________.  
> Insight / fix: ________.

### 8. AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action)

**What it is.** A four-part sequence: earn attention, deepen interest, create a want, ask for a step.

**Origin.** Disputed. For much of the twentieth century it was credited to E. St. Elmo Lewis, via Edward Strong’s 1925 account. A 2024 historical review by Akinori Iwamoto, [The origin of AIDA: Who invented and formulated the AIDA model?](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jmhr/3/2/3_150/_article/-char/en) (*Journal of Marketing History Research*, 2024), argues that account is weakly evidenced and points instead to Arthur Sheldon and Frank Dukesmith. We mark the origin as **disputed** and do not pretend to settle it.

**Caution.** In this guide, **Desire** is “I want to try that”, and **Action** is “I’ll cook it tonight” or “I’ll comment”. Not “buy”.

**Fill in.**

> Attention: ________.  
> Interest: ________.  
> Desire (to try, not to buy): ________.  
> Action: ________.

### 9. The Hero’s Journey

**Theory.** Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949), described a common mythic pattern often called the monomyth. Christopher Vogler’s *The Writer’s Journey* is a well-known screenwriting adaptation. These are books; we are not quoting them at length.

**When it helps.** Longer stories, vlogs with a genuine change, or a “I went out to do X, it went wrong, I came back different” walk. Too heavy for every 20-second tip.

**Fill in (compressed).**

> Ordinary world: ________.  
> I wanted: ________.  
> I went into: ________.  
> It cost me: ________.  
> I came back, and what changed was: ________.

### 10. Dan Harmon’s Story Circle

**Source.** Dan Harmon wrote an eight-step circle (a character is in a zone of comfort; they want something; they enter an unfamiliar situation; they adapt; they get what they wanted; they pay a price; they return; they have changed) as Channel 101’s “Story Structure 101”. A 2011 [Vulture piece](https://www.vulture.com/2011/03/the-dan-harmon-school-of-comedy-writing.html) quotes the post. **To do in a refresh:** open Harmon’s original post if the URL is still live and cite that as primary.

**Fill in.** You / Need / Go / Search / Find / Take / Return / Change — one short clause each.

**Maya (a walk).** You: she comes home tired. Need: she wants to leave the flat without a “proper plan”. Go: the canal towpath. Search: she almost turns back at the first bridge. Find: a twenty-minute loop. Take: she is late for the last shop. Return: the same kitchen. Change: the walk is now the start of dinner, not a thing she failed to do.

### 11. The Story Spine

**Source.** Kenn Adams’s improv tool: “Once upon a time… Every day… Until one day… Because of that… Until finally… And ever since then.” Pixar has used it in training; Adams is the creator. NPR interviewed him in June 2026: [Meet the creator of the Story Spine](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/23/nx-s1-5750619/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories).

**Fill in.** Use Adams’s prompts, one line each, then cut it down to a 30-second voiceover.

### 12. Peak-end as a design check (not a script formula)

**Theory.** Fredrickson and Kahneman, 1993 — see [chapter 6](06-attention.md). Not a monologue structure. After you draft, ask: where is the most intense moment, and does the ending keep the promise?

**Fill in.**

> Peak moment (second ___): ________.  
> Ending line: ________.  
> Do they match the hook? Yes / not yet.

## Spoken script versus caption

Write the **spoken** script first, for the ear. Then write a **caption** that still makes sense if someone never presses play: one or two sentences plus, if you want, a hashtag limit from [chapter 8](08-text-hashtags-thumbnails.md). Do not paste the whole monologue into the caption box.

## Maya

She drafts the tomato-pan video three ways (curiosity gap; promise–break–frustrate–solve; but/therefore) and records the second, because it matches how she actually burned the onions. She keeps a CTA that asks for the viewer’s version, not a follow.

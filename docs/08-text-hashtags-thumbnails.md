# 8 · Text, hashtags, titles and thumbnails

> **In one sentence:** packaging is how a stranger understands the promise — on the screen, in the caption, and in the YouTube title and thumbnail — and it should be honest.

## New words

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **On-screen text** | Words drawn on the video itself. |
| **Captions / subtitles** | Words that follow the speech, for people watching without sound or who want to read along. |
| **Hashtag** | A #word that can group posts or appear in search. |
| **Thumbnail** | The still image YouTube (and some other surfaces) show before someone plays the video. |
| **Title** | The name of the video, especially on YouTube. |
| **CTR** (click-through rate) | Of the people who saw the thumbnail (an **impression**), the share who clicked. Official YouTube analytics define these in Help: [Understand your YouTube content performance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281?hl=en) (Studio Help also covers this under content performance). |

## Text on screen, and captions

People often watch with the sound off. Official tools exist:

- Instagram: [Add text and text-to-speech to your reel](https://help.instagram.com/3544819209117630)
- YouTube: [Shorts editing tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13380879?hl=en) (Text on a Short), and [Add subtitles & captions](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2734796?hl=en) for longer videos
- YouTube also documents [automatic captioning](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6373554?hl=en); always read them back, because automatic captions are often wrong

**House rules (ours, not a platform law):**

- Prefer captions that match what you said, not a second essay.
- Keep lines short (a useful rule of thumb is about six words a line — a house rule, not a standard).
- Place text where buttons will not cover it.
- Do not fill the frame with words. Instagram’s Help on [what kind of reels you may see](https://help.instagram.com/1525585517644948) says reels may be shown less often if, among other things, “the majority of the image [is] covered by text”, or if they are muted, blurry, low resolution, or carry watermarks. That is Instagram’s own language, not a guarantee.

This is also where [chapter 6](06-attention.md) on **cognitive load** lands: pictures + spoken words + a paragraph of different text is a lot. Captions that *are* the speech are usually enough.

## Hashtags

**Instagram.** Official Help, [Use hashtags on Instagram](https://help.instagram.com/351460621611097), says you can add hashtags in the caption or comments, and — as of the page we opened on 20 Nov 2026 — **“You can use up to 5 tags on a post. If you include more than 5 tags on a single photo/video, your comment won’t post.”** Older advice you may still see (“use 30 hashtags”) is out of date if this Help page is current. Recheck the [platform snapshot](platform-snapshot.md).

**YouTube.** Official Help, [Find playlists & videos using hashtags](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6390658?hl=en), explains that you can add hashtags in a title or description, and that up to three relevant hashtags may be shown above the video title. It also warns against adding unrelated tags or large numbers of them (the page we opened mentioned a limit in that region of **60**; read the live page before you quote a number in class, and check the snapshot if it changes).

**TikTok and X.** Hashtags exist and can appear in search; treat them as labels, not magic. We did not find, in this draft, a single official “use exactly N hashtags” rule we are willing to invent.

**House rule:** a few accurate tags beat a list of wishes. Never tag a video as something it is not.

## YouTube titles and thumbnails

YouTube’s official [Thumbnail & title tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en) are the base:

- **Titles:** be accurate; be succinct; put important words near the beginning; use ALL CAPS and emoji sparingly.
- **Thumbnails:** know whether you are talking to people who already know you or to strangers; keep the image readable at small size; do not make the design too complex; if you add text, use a readable font.

YouTube also warns against misleading, shocking or otherwise policy-breaking packaging in [performance FAQ](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/141805?hl=en).

You can **A/B test** titles and thumbnails in Studio where the feature is available: [A/B test titles & thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400?hl=en). YouTube has also described **Dynamic Thumbnails** and related tests in official creator material (for example coverage around Made on YouTube / Creator Insider). If a feature is not in your account yet, you are not doing it wrong; roll-outs are uneven. See the [platform snapshot](platform-snapshot.md).

An official conversation worth watching for *how YouTube talks about packaging* is [A deep thumbnail discussion with Chucky (MrBeast) and Todd](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HP-rqigfw2s) on Creator Insider. We point to it as a primary YouTube conversation, not as “do what MrBeast does”, and not as monetisation advice.

**Impressions and CTR** only make sense together: a loud thumbnail that gets clicks and immediate skips is not a win. YouTube’s own analytics help covers impressions and click-through rate in content performance reports ([Understand your YouTube content performance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281?hl=en) / Studio Help).

## Maya

She writes a caption that still works if the sound is off: “The Tuesday dinner I actually cook: one pan, a tin of tomatoes, no fourteen-ingredient recipe.” She uses two Instagram hashtags that describe the post, not ten. For a later YouTube Short she uses a cover still of the pan and a title that is the same promise, not a riddle.

## Exercise

For your next post, write: on-screen caption lines, the written caption, and (if YouTube) a title plus a one-sentence description of the cover. Check each against the promise in the script. If they do not match, change the packaging, not the truth.
